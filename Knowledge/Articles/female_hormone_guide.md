MENSTRUAL CYCLE BIOCHEMISTRY
The menstrual cycle goes as follows:
Follicular Phase (Day ~1-14): Estrogen & FSH rise, stimulating the growth & maturation of ovarian follicles. The endometrial lining starts to thicken in preparation for potential implantation. Estrogen gradually peaks toward the end of this phase, triggering a surge in LH, which sets ovulation in motion. This phase is often tied to higher energy, elevated mood, sharper cognition, & better insulin sensitivity thanks to rising estrogen.
Ovulation (Around Day 14): The LH surge leads to the release of the dominant egg from the ovary. Estrogen is at its peak, & a slight rise in testosterone can boost libido, energy, & confidence. The egg then travels down the fallopian tube, marking the most fertile window of the cycle.
Luteal Phase (Day ~15-28): After ovulation, the ruptured follicle transforms into the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone & some estrogen. Progesterone takes the lead here, stabilizing the endometrial lining & slightly increasing body temperature. If fertilization doesn’t happen, the corpus luteum breaks down, causing a drop in both progesterone & estrogen. This hormonal dip can trigger PMS symptoms—think mood swings, cravings, bloating, & fatigue. If a female becomes pregnant, progesterone continues to rise.
Menstruation (Day 1 of the next cycle): With estrogen & progesterone at their lowest, the uterine lining sheds, kickstarting the next cycle. This is the body's natural reset button, often bringing lower energy, more introspection, & a shift in mood as hormone levels bottom out.
Women require a healthy menstrual cycle from start-to-finish—this includes the small rise in FSH, followed by the rise in estrogen (follicular phase), followed by the LH surge (ovulation), followed by a rise in progesterone (luteal phase). This rise in progesterone will continue if one is pregnant, otherwise it will go back down, the period occurs, & the cycle resets. The egg releases about 10-12 hrs after LH peak, & the LH surges roughly 34-36 hrs prior to ovulation. The egg released lives only 4-24 hrs, if one is in optimal health, this is closer to 24 hrs.
So as you can see, generally there is a short window for actual pregnancy, however this isn't always the case. Women can test fertility by checking their blood work (estradiol, progesterone, LH/FSH, etc.) at various times of the cycle (usually Day 2-3, & Day 25-28, around 4-5 days after luteal phase begins), as well using an ovulation predictor kit (OPK) urine test to determine LH surge (it can be tested multiple times to determine how long the surge goes for).
One of the best ways for females to determine ovulation is to monitor their body temperature. Your body temperature naturally changes a tiny bit throughout your menstrual cycle. It’s lower in the first part of your cycle, and then rises when you ovulate. For most people, 96°– 98° Fahrenheit is their typical temperature before ovulation. After you ovulate, it goes up to 97°–99°F — about four-tenths of one degree higher than your usual temperature.
To use the temperature method (AKA basal temperature method), you must take your temperature the same way, at the same time, every single day—& write it on a fertility awareness chart. You can get fertility awareness method (FAM) charts from HERE.
This entire cycle is a rhythmic dance of hormones that not only governs fertility but also deeply impacts physical, mental, & spiritual health throughout the month. This is exactly why metabolism, appetite, libido, emotions, digestion, & cognition constantly shift throughout the cycle.
For example, the LH surge driven by heightened estradiol activity also leads to increased mRNA of melanocortin-3 receptors (MC3r), as well as estradiol & progesterone directly increase alpha-MSH—which acts on MC3r to enhance libido. The estradiol in itself is also potent at enhancing libido, which makes sense as a woman during ovulation, should biologically want to have as much sex as possible in order to create life.
You see this also with progesterone fluctuations during a cycle & during pregnancy, progesterone slows down gut motility—allowing for more nutrients to be absorbed for the fetus to capitalize on. It can significantly impact gut motility, often leading to bloating due to its ability to relax the smooth muscles of the gastrointestinal tract, slowing down digestion & causing constipation or feelings of fullness, particularly during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle when progesterone levels are high. It also leads to water retention to allow for more infrared light absorption for a higher density of coherent water networks for the growing baby & future mother to take advantage of. Water retention can also lead to bloating, especially if vasopressin & aldosterone-cortisol cycles are dysregulated (very much in the modern world).

ESTROGEN & PROGESTERONE
Just touching on estrogen:
There are 3 types of estrogen: E1 (estrone), E2 (estradiol), & E3 (estriol).
E2 is the most active & potent form of estrogen in the body.
Excess E1 & E2 can promote cancer, while E3 offers protective, anti-carcinogenic effects.
Estrogen also gets metabolized in the liver into various methoxy & hydroxy forms (we will touch on this more below):
2-OH (2-hydroxyestrone) → protective, anti-proliferative.
4-OH (4-hydroxyestrone) → potentially carcinogenic if not properly methylated.
16-OH (16α-hydroxyestrone) → proliferative & linked to increased cancer risk.
Estrogen receptors:
ER-alpha — pro-inflammatory & proliferative (linked to cancer risk).
ER-beta — anti-inflammatory & protective.
GPER — rapid, non-genomic signaling affecting metabolism & immune function.
Xenoestrogens (found in plastics, pesticides, & personal care products) are potent ER-alpha agonists, driving inflammation & carcinogenic effects.
Phytoestrogens (found in flaxseeds, soy, legumes, etc.) modulate estrogen receptors, which favours ER-beta, leading to protective effects.
Progesterone is made from pregnenolone, but it can be shunted into cortisol. It acts on progesterone receptors (PRs) which tend to have anti-stress, estrogen-balancing, neuroprotective, & anti-inflammatory effects.
Progesterone utilizes vitamin C, vitamin B6, magnesium, & zinc to enhance its synthesis. There are also certain compounds such as vitex & bladderwrack seaweed that can promote a healthy estradiol/progesterone balance.

NEUROSTEROIDS, BRAIN LH RECEPTORS & LIBIDO
What’s often overlooked in conventional hormone models is the influence of neurosteroids—compounds like pregnenolone, DHEA, allopregnanolone, and their sulfated derivatives—which are synthesized directly in the brain by neurons, astrocytes, and glial cells.
Unlike classical sex hormones, these neurosteroids can act locally, rapidly, and non-genomically on receptors like GABA-A, NMDA, and dopamine receptors to regulate mood, behavior, and most relevantly here—sexual desire and function.
Research has shown that LH receptors are not only expressed in the ovaries—but also locally in the brain, including hypothalamic and limbic regions responsible for sexual motivation, emotion, and autonomic output. These brain-based LH receptors are responsive to both systemic and local neurosteroid signals.
During periods of hormonal flux—especially around ovulation or luteal shifts—locally synthesized neurosteroids like pregnenolone sulfate and allopregnanolone can act on brain circuits like the medial preoptic area (mPOA), ventromedial hypothalamus (VMN), and nucleus accumbens to drive sexual receptivity, reward, and approach behavior.
These are the same areas enriched in dopamine and LH receptor activity, suggesting that libido is not just hormonally driven from the ovaries, but also by a neuroendocrine axis between neurosteroids and brain-based LH signaling.
What this means is that even in the absence of significant peripheral LH changes, the brain can generate its own feedback loops using neurosteroids—to regulate sexual arousal, motivation, and even reproductive timing. The integration of light, redox status, melatonin, dopamine, and GABAergic tone in this system makes it incredibly sensitive to circadian rhythm, emotional state, and environmental cues.
So if you’re experiencing fluctuations in libido, mood, or energy—especially around ovulation—it may not just be your blood hormone levels. It could be the orchestration of local neurosteroid signaling via brain-based LH receptors and their crosstalk with neurotransmitter systems like dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, and oxytocin. Understanding this system opens up deeper doors for resolving PMS, PMDD, anxiety, low libido, and brain fog—not with more hormones, but by regulating neurosteroid tone, circadian alignment, and environmental signals.

ESTROGEN METABOLISM:
Methylation (via COMT) converts harmful hydroxy estrogens into methoxy forms, followed by glucuronidation or sulfation, reducing cancer risk. Poor methylation (requires B2, B3, B6, B9, B12, magnesium, zinc, choline, methionine, serine/glycine, vitamin A, & copper) can lead to the buildup of harmful estrogen metabolites (I mentioned this in the webinar above).
Metabolite Pathways:
Estrogen metabolism occurs in three distinct phases in the liver, impacting its detoxification and potential health effects.
Phase 1 – CYP450 Enzyme Activity (Hydroxylation):
CYP1A1/1A2: Converts estrone/estradiol into 2-OH-E1/E2 → least carcinogenic → COMT methylates to 2-methoxy-E1/E2 (protective, anti-proliferative).
CYP1B1 → Converts estrone/estradiol into 4-OH-E1/E2 → most carcinogenic if not methylated → forms DNA-damaging quinones → COMT methylates to 4-methoxy-E1/E2. NQO1 & GST assist in detoxifying quinones, preventing DNA damage.
CYP3A4 → Converts to 16-OH-E1/E2 → binds tightly to ER-alpha → highly estrogenic & proliferative → further metabolized to E3 (estriol) by 17β-HSD (less carcinogenic).
Phase 2 – Conjugation (Detoxification):
Converts Phase 1 metabolites into water-soluble forms via:
Methylation (COMT) – critical for deactivating 2-OH/4-OH forms.
Methylation requires: B2, B3, B6, B9, B12, magnesium, zinc, choline, methionine, serine/glycine, vitamin A, & copper. Issues with MTHFR can affect this pathway.
Glucuronidation (UGT) — for urinary & stool excretion via glucuronic acid.
Sulfation (SULT) — another detox route which requires UV light, sulfur, molybdenum, & any sulfur-derived amino acids.
Glutathione Conjugation (GST) — binds reactive quinones, glutathione requires cysteine, glutamic acid, & glycine.
Phase 3 – Elimination (Excretion):
Water-soluble estrogens are excreted through bile → intestines → stool + kidneys → urine
Beta-glucuronidase from gut bacteria can reverse conjugation, reactivating estrogen → high B-glucuronidase = poor clearance — use calcium D-glucarate or regular consumption of grapefruit/pomelo (contains glucuronic acid) to inhibit.
Negative-gram bacteria infections cause huge elevations in B-glucuronidase activity. So optimizing gut health is key. A dead giveaway is if you're sensitive to FODMAP foods, alongside suboptimal blood work.

THE DANCE BETWEEN FOLLICULAR, THECA, & LUTEAL CELLS
This intricate coordination between follicular, theca, & luteal cells is essential for proper follicle development, ovulation, & progesterone production.
Disruptions in any of these factors can lead to hormonal imbalances, anovulation, or conditions like PCOS, directly impacting fertility & overall reproductive health.
When there's an impairment in the conversion of follicular cells to theca cells, it disrupts normal androgen-to-estrogen conversion, leading to an accumulation of androgens like testosterone. This is because theca cells primarily produce androgens (e.g., androstenedione and testosterone) which are then aromatized into estrogens by granulosa cells. If this process is disrupted—often seen in conditions like PCOS—the excess androgens aren't properly converted, resulting in elevated testosterone levels & contributing to symptoms like acne, hirsutism, & anovulation.
Many factors ensure the follicular cells, theca cells, & luteal cells of a female are working in harmony in the ovaries:
Hormonal balance (FSH, LH, estrogen, progesterone, androgens)
Healthy blood flow to the ovaries & follicles
Proper mitochondrial function
Optimal nutrient supply (cholesterol, vitamins, minerals)
Cellular communication (gap junctions like Cx43)
Enzyme activity (CYP11A1, CYP17, CYP19A1, 17HSD)
Effective signaling between granulosa and theca cells
Proper circadian rhythms & hormonal pulses (e.g., LH pulses)
Healthy oocyte environment (from granulosa cell support)
Absence of oxidative stress or inflammation
Balanced insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism
Efficient feedback loops between hypothalamus, pituitary, and ovaries
Supportive role of oocyte-derived molecules (e.g., TGFβ, BMPs, AMH)

BIO-IDENTICAL HORMONES & HORMONAL SUPPLEMENTS
When various strategies above aren’t enough to fully balance hormones, bio-identical hormone replacement therapy (BHRT) & targeted hormonal supplements can be powerful tools for restoring equilibrium. If you asked me a year ago if this was the answer, I would tend to disagree, however, it's context-dependent. These therapies aim to mimic the body’s natural hormone production, providing individualized support based on specific hormonal imbalances.
This is what is normally used:
Progesterone (Micronized or Topical Creams): Supports luteal phase deficiencies, estrogen dominance, & PMS/PMDD. Enhances GABAergic tone, promoting calmness, mood enhancement, & improves sleep. Often dosed in the luteal phase typically.
Pregnenolone: The precursor to ALL sex hormones, as well as some neurosteroids of its own. Best to take as long as one has optimized light environment, sleep, stress management, & nutrition.
Estradiol (E2): Used in cases of low estrogen (e.g., menopause, premature ovarian failure). However, recent studies have shown that estrogen is actually still high in women who are post-menopausal—it's only low in the serum.
DHEA (Dehydroepiandrosterone): DHEA is mostly estrogenic in males, but in females, it's actually mostly tied to androgenic activity & conversion. Best to be taken orally.

ENVIRONMENTAL TOXINS & DETOXIFICATION
Another thing we have to consider is our toxin load. Our world is filled with chemicals—from pollutants in the air, pesticides in our food, to mold & dust indoors, to plastics & heavy metals in our everyday environment. All of these have slightly different biochemical effects, but the results are all somewhat the same; chronic inflammation (usually via NF-kB pathway & excess estrogenic activity) & multiple health issues. BPAs contain xenoestrogens which activate ERa very strongly, as well as phthalates being able to inhibit 5-alpha reductase, as examples.
This can span from hormonal imbalances, gut dysbiosis, fatty liver, autoimmune conditions, neurological disorders, metabolic syndrome, & more. Minimizing exposure is one thing, whilst we also need to support both our redox & detox pathways is essential to maintain optimal health. Again this is best done by copying nature & mitigate the modern world. Sometimes in this case though, man-made problems require man-made solutions, so supplementation, herbals, functional foods, & biohacks may be necessary to mitigate. It depends on the context, as always.

Using natural cleaning products free from synthetic fragrances, phthalates, and harmful chemicals.
Using natural soaps, deodorants, and personal care products to reduce contact with endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) like parabens and aluminum.
Avoiding plastic water bottles and plastic cutting boards to minimize BPA, BPS, and microplastic exposure.
Choosing glass, stainless steel, or ceramic for food and drink storage.
Avoiding polyester and synthetic clothing, especially underwear, as they can leach microplastics and disrupt thermal regulation.
Opting for organic cotton, hemp, or bamboo fabrics for clothing and bedding.
Using non-toxic cookware like stainless steel, cast iron, or ceramic instead of Teflon or non-stick coatings.
Avoiding air fresheners, scented candles, and synthetic fragrances that release volatile organic compounds (VOCs).
Choosing natural or mineral-based sunscreens to avoid chemical UV filters like oxybenzone and octinoxate.
Filtering tap water with high-quality carbon or reverse osmosis filters to reduce heavy metals, chlorine, fluoride, and pesticide residues.
Using HEPA air purifiers to minimize airborne toxins, dust, and mold spores.
Reducing exposure to flame retardants by choosing furniture and mattresses made without chemical treatments.
Limiting canned foods due to BPA-lined cans and opting for fresh or frozen alternatives.
Avoiding processed foods high in preservatives, artificial dyes, and chemical additives.

Ensuring adequate hydration with mineral-rich water to support kidney detox pathways.
Consuming cruciferous vegetables (like broccoli, cauliflower, and kale) for their sulforaphane content, aiding liver detoxification.
Supporting methylation, sulfation, glucuronidation, & glutathionylation, as well as phase II liver detox with B vitamins, choline, taurine, glycine, bile acid support, and magnesium.
